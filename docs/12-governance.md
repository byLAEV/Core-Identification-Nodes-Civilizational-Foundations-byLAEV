# 12. Governance

## Core Identification Nodes — Civilizational Foundations byLAEV

**Document Classification:** Foundational Governance Framework  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Status:** Draft

## 12.1. Distributed Participation

The Governance Framework establishes how participants contribute to, make decisions about, maintain, and evolve the Core Identification Nodes byLAEV ecosystem.

Governance is distinct from technical consensus. A consensus protocol may coordinate a specific shared system state, but governance determines who may propose rules, who may participate in decisions, how responsibilities are assigned, how disputes are addressed, and how changes become accepted project or network rules.

The project investigates a distributed and interoperable architecture. Its governance must therefore support participation by independent nodes and institutions without assuming that every participant has the same role, authority, resources, or legal status.

### 12.1.1. Governance as a System of Rules and Responsibilities

Governance is the set of arrangements through which the ecosystem establishes, applies, reviews, and changes its rules.

It includes:

- The principles and foundational commitments of the project.
- The roles and responsibilities of participants.
- The procedures for proposing and deciding changes.
- The rules governing protocols and shared infrastructure.
- The treatment of disputes and unresolved differences.
- The relationships with institutions and applicable legal frameworks.
- The methods for accountability, transparency, and review.

Governance is not limited to a single committee, voting mechanism, software process, or technical role. It is the overall structure through which decisions and responsibilities are organized.

### 12.1.2. Distributed Participation

Distributed participation means that defined participants may contribute to governance within the scope of their roles and the decisions for which they are eligible.

Participation may include:

- Proposing changes.
- Reviewing proposals.
- Providing evidence or technical analysis.
- Participating in decisions.
- Operating or maintaining network components.
- Reporting problems or discrepancies.
- Requesting review of a decision.
- Contributing to institutional and international cooperation.

Participation does not imply that every participant must have identical authority over every subject. Governance must define the decision domain and eligibility conditions for each type of decision.

### 12.1.3. Participation Categories

The ecosystem may include several categories of participants:

- **Individual participants:** people participating through defined nodes or other authorized means.
- **Technical contributors:** people contributing software, documentation, research, or operational work.
- **Node operators:** participants responsible for maintaining specific technical nodes or services.
- **Institutional participants:** organizations or public bodies participating under their own authority and applicable agreements.
- **Standards and protocol contributors:** participants developing or reviewing shared definitions and specifications.
- **Governance participants:** participants authorized to take part in defined governance processes.
- **Independent reviewers:** persons or organizations examining claims, implementations, or decisions without being the sole authors of the material under review.

These categories may overlap, but their roles must be explicit. A person may contribute technically without having institutional authority. An organization may operate a node without representing every person whose information it processes.

### 12.1.4. Participation and Representation

The project must distinguish a participant from the technical account, node, or endpoint through which participation occurs.

One person may operate multiple accounts or nodes. An account may be shared or operated by an organization. A node may represent a technical process rather than a human participant.

Therefore, a GitHub account or another technical account must not automatically be counted as one unique human participant for governance purposes.

Any representation or eligibility method must state what it proves, what it does not prove, and how multiple or organizational representations are handled.

### 12.1.5. Distributed Does Not Mean Unstructured

Distributed participation requires shared procedures and clearly defined decision boundaries.

Without a defined process, multiple participants may claim authority over the same decision, conflicting protocol versions may emerge, or important responsibilities may remain unassigned.

The governance framework must specify:

- Which decisions are local to a node.
- Which decisions are shared within a protocol or network domain.
- Which decisions concern the project's foundational documentation.
- Which matters remain within institutional or jurisdictional authority.
- Which matters require formal cooperation agreements.

### 12.1.6. Governance and Polycentric Organization

The project may draw on polycentric governance as an analytical reference for understanding systems in which multiple decision centers operate with degrees of independence while coordinating through rules and relationships.

Elinor Ostrom's research examined institutional arrangements associated with robust governance of common-pool resources. Her work emphasizes that institutional design depends on context and that principles observed in one domain should not be copied as universal rules without examining the underlying conditions.

Core Identification Nodes byLAEV may use polycentric governance as a comparative framework, but must not assume that findings from common-pool resource institutions automatically validate the governance of a global information network.

**Governance principle:** distributed participation should be organized through explicit domains of authority, defined responsibilities, and mechanisms for cooperation and accountability.

## 12.2. Consensus Rules

Consensus rules define how agreement is established for specific network and governance decisions.

The project distinguishes **technical consensus**, **protocol governance decisions**, **project governance decisions**, and **institutional or legal recognition**. These are related but different forms of agreement.

### 12.2.1. Technical Consensus

Technical consensus is agreement among eligible nodes about a defined shared state, ordering, or protocol-governed result.

It is a system operation. Its authority is limited to the scope of the relevant protocol and participating nodes.

Technical consensus does not independently establish:

- The factual truth of an underlying Human Event.
- The legal authority of a submitting participant.
- The legal validity of a civil-registration record.
- Agreement among all institutions or jurisdictions.
- The legitimacy of every governance decision.

### 12.2.2. Governance Consensus

Governance consensus concerns agreement on rules, standards, protocol amendments, or decisions affecting the ecosystem.

A governance procedure must specify:

- The decision domain.
- The participants eligible to propose or decide.
- The information required to evaluate a proposal.
- The deliberation period or review process.
- The decision rule.
- The threshold or condition for adoption.
- The recording and publication of the outcome.
- The effective date and transition arrangements.
- The available review or appeal procedure.

The phrase "network consensus" must never substitute for these definitions.

### 12.2.3. Decision Domains

Different decision domains may require different procedures.

Potential domains include:

1. **Foundational governance:** changes to axioms, principles, or core conceptual relationships.
2. **Documentation governance:** acceptance and revision of official project documents.
3. **Protocol governance:** adoption and amendment of communication and operational rules.
4. **Technical operations:** implementation choices, maintenance, and deployment.
5. **Network state:** decisions coordinated by technical consensus protocols.
6. **Institutional cooperation:** agreements and commitments made by participating organizations under their own authority.

One voting or consensus mechanism should not automatically govern every domain.

### 12.2.4. Eligibility and Representation

Eligibility must be defined by decision domain and role.

The project should not assume that one technical account equals one human vote. Nor should it assume that every technical node, institutional participant, software operator, and individual contributor should have identical voting weight for every decision.

Possible eligibility models include:

- Role-based participation.
- One vote per verified organizational member within a defined institutional process.
- One vote per admitted technical node for a technical protocol decision.
- Domain-specific representation.
- Delegated review or approval for specialized technical or institutional matters.
- Multi-stage decisions requiring agreement from more than one participant category.

These are possible governance models for evaluation, not adopted voting rules. Any model must address duplicate representation, conflicts of interest, unequal access, concentration of influence, and the basis on which a participant is eligible.

### 12.2.5. Proposal and Deliberation

A governance proposal should contain:

- A stable proposal reference.
- The issue or requirement motivating it.
- The proposed change.
- The affected documents, protocols, or components.
- Supporting evidence and reasoning.
- Expected benefits and potential consequences.
- Compatibility and migration implications.
- The decision domain and eligible participants.
- The proposed adoption procedure.
- The criteria by which success may later be evaluated.

Participants should have a meaningful opportunity to examine material proposals before a decision is finalized.

### 12.2.6. Decision Rules and Thresholds

The decision rule must be appropriate to the consequence and scope of the decision.

Possible methods include simple majority, qualified majority, consensus-seeking procedures, representative approval, or approval by multiple defined decision domains.

The project has not adopted a universal threshold. A threshold must not be selected merely for convenience. It should be justified by the decision's significance, participation model, risk of capture, and need for operational continuity.

Changes to foundational principles may require a more demanding procedure than routine technical maintenance. Emergency operational actions may require a separate, narrowly bounded procedure with documented review afterward.

### 12.2.7. Dissent and Minority Positions

A decision record should preserve material dissent where it affects interpretation, implementation, or future review.

Dissent does not automatically invalidate a decision reached under the established procedure. Equally, a majority decision must not erase relevant technical evidence, legal limitations, or documented concerns.

The governance framework should provide a way to record unresolved positions and identify whether they affect the decision's scope or validity.

### 12.2.8. Decision Record

Every significant governance decision should be documented with:

- Proposal reference.
- Decision domain.
- Eligible participants or governing body.
- Procedure used.
- Decision result.
- Recorded dissent or abstentions where relevant.
- Rationale and supporting material.
- Effective version or date.
- Required follow-up actions.
- Review or appeal status.

A decision without a traceable record may be difficult to interpret or verify later.

## 12.3. Participants' Rights and Responsibilities

The governance framework must establish the rights and responsibilities that apply to participation within the project and any future network.

The rights in this section are **project governance commitments**. They do not replace or redefine legal rights established by national law or international instruments.

### 12.3.1. Participation Rights

Subject to the relevant role and governance rules, participants should have the ability to:

- Access the public project documentation and applicable participation rules.
- Understand the responsibilities and limitations of the role they undertake.
- Submit proposals and provide relevant information.
- Participate in reviews and decisions for which they are eligible.
- Receive notice of material changes affecting their participation.
- Obtain a reasoned explanation of relevant governance decisions.
- Raise concerns, report discrepancies, and request review under defined procedures.
- Withdraw from voluntary participation under the applicable rules.
- Access or correct information about their own participation where the system and applicable law provide for it.

These commitments must be translated into operational procedures and must account for the capabilities and responsibilities of different participant categories.

### 12.3.2. Information and Transparency Rights

Participants should be able to determine:

- Which protocol version applies to their operations.
- Which role and permissions are assigned to them.
- Which information is required for a defined operation.
- Which participant or system is responsible for a decision.
- What a network status means.
- What a record or sequence reference does and does not establish.
- How a dispute or correction can be initiated.

Transparency does not require public disclosure of all personal or institutionally restricted information. The governance framework must distinguish transparency of rules and decisions from unrestricted access to underlying records.

### 12.3.3. Responsibilities of Participants

Participants must, within the scope of their roles:

- Follow applicable project rules and protocol specifications.
- Provide accurate information to the best of their knowledge and disclose relevant uncertainty.
- Avoid presenting technical acknowledgements as legal determinations.
- Preserve the provenance and context of information they communicate.
- Respect applicable access and disclosure conditions.
- Avoid misrepresenting their authority or the status of their records.
- Cooperate with justified correction and reconciliation processes.
- Disclose relevant conflicts of interest in governance decisions.
- Document operational changes that affect shared functions.
- Respect the rights and legitimate interests of other participants.

Responsibilities must be proportionate to the actual role. A participant who only contributes public documentation should not be subject to the same operational responsibilities as an institution that maintains a record service.

### 12.3.4. Responsibilities of Node Operators

Operators of technical nodes or services may have additional responsibilities, including:

- Maintaining the declared function of the service.
- Identifying supported protocol versions.
- Communicating service status and relevant interruptions.
- Preserving operational records required by the role.
- Applying approved updates and communicating breaking changes.
- Reporting material operational discrepancies.
- Maintaining a documented process for handover or retirement.

The detailed operator obligations must be established in the applicable technical and operational agreements.

### 12.3.5. Responsibilities of Institutional Participants

An institution participating in the network remains responsible for acting within its own authority and applicable legal framework.

It must not imply that network participation transfers its legal obligations to the project or to another node.

Institutional participants should specify which records and attestations they are authorized to provide and what legal or administrative status those outputs have within their jurisdiction.

### 12.3.6. Conflicts of Interest

A participant may have a direct or indirect interest in a governance decision.

The governance process should define when disclosure is required, how the conflict is recorded, and whether the participant may deliberate, vote, approve, or review the matter.

The treatment of a conflict must be proportionate. Not every professional or institutional relationship automatically disqualifies participation, but undisclosed interests that materially influence a decision undermine accountability.

### 12.3.7. Accountability and Proportionate Consequences

Where a participant fails to meet defined responsibilities, the governance framework should provide proportionate procedures.

Possible responses may include clarification, corrective action, temporary restriction of a specific network function, suspension under defined conditions, or termination of participation.

Any such response must:

- Be based on a defined rule.
- Identify the conduct and relevant evidence.
- Be proportionate to the issue.
- Provide an opportunity to respond where appropriate.
- Be recorded.
- Include a review or appeal route where applicable.

The project must not create arbitrary penalties or imply that network measures determine a person's legal status outside the network.

## 12.4. Protocol Evolution

Protocol evolution governs how the ecosystem's shared operational rules are proposed, reviewed, approved, implemented, and retired.

The objective is to allow the architecture to improve without creating uncontrolled fragmentation or silently changing the meaning of existing records and operations.

### 12.4.1. Evolution Principles

Protocol evolution must preserve:

- Traceability of changes.
- Clear version identification.
- Compatibility analysis.
- Defined responsibilities for approval.
- A public or otherwise appropriately accessible change rationale.
- Conformance tests for the revised protocol.
- Documented migration procedures.
- Historical interpretability of previous versions.

### 12.4.2. Change Proposal

A protocol change proposal should include:

1. Protocol name and current version.
2. Problem or requirement motivating the change.
3. Proposed modification.
4. Affected operations, messages, states, or data structures.
5. Compatibility and dependency analysis.
6. Potential consequences for participants and institutions.
7. Test plan and acceptance criteria.
8. Proposed transition schedule.
9. Review findings.
10. Required decision and approval domain.

### 12.4.3. Change Categories

Changes should be classified according to their effect:

- **Editorial change:** clarifies wording without changing meaning.
- **Clarifying change:** defines an existing behavior more precisely.
- **Compatible change:** adds or modifies behavior without invalidating supported existing operations.
- **Breaking change:** requires dependent implementations or participants to modify their behavior.
- **Foundational change:** alters a core principle, definition, or relationship.
- **Emergency change:** addresses an identified operational issue under a narrowly defined procedure.

A change category determines the review and transition requirements.

### 12.4.4. Adoption and Activation

Approval of a protocol change and activation within an implementation are separate events.

The governance record should identify when a change is approved, when it becomes available, which participants have adopted it, and which versions remain supported.

A protocol update must not silently alter the meaning of an existing record or sequence position. If a semantic change is necessary, the affected information and migration relationships must be documented.

### 12.4.5. Backward Compatibility and Migration

The governance process should require explicit analysis of:

- Previous protocol versions.
- Existing records and references.
- Participating nodes that cannot update immediately.
- Translation or compatibility mechanisms.
- Deprecation periods.
- Data and service migration.
- Expected operational interruption, if any.

Backward compatibility should be maintained where appropriate, but it must not be treated as an absolute requirement when it conflicts with a justified correction, legal obligation, or necessary change.

### 12.4.6. Emergency Changes

An emergency process may be required to address a clearly identified operational failure or an urgent issue affecting the network.

Emergency authority must be narrowly defined, time-limited, and subject to retrospective review.

An emergency process must not become a routine means of bypassing governance or making permanent foundational changes without the normal review and approval process.

### 12.4.7. Protocol Registry and Change History

The project should maintain a registry of protocols and their versions, status, dependencies, approval records, and change history.

The registry may begin within the repository and later be integrated with technical tooling as required.

## 12.5. Discrepancy Resolution

Discrepancy resolution provides the governance framework for addressing disagreements that cannot be resolved solely through routine technical operations.

The governance process must distinguish technical inconsistencies, data disputes, institutional disagreements, and legal disputes because they may require different procedures and authorities.

### 12.5.1. Types of Dispute

Disputes may concern:

- A node's role or participation status.
- Interpretation of a protocol.
- A governance decision.
- The content or provenance of a communicated History.
- The relationship between two record references.
- A disputed or corrected event representation.
- A sequence inclusion or ordering result.
- The responsibilities of a node operator.
- The interpretation of an institutional attestation.
- An alleged breach of participation rules.

### 12.5.2. Resolution Levels

The project should consider a graduated resolution structure:

1. **Technical clarification:** resolve an issue by explaining or correcting the interpretation of a technical specification.
2. **Operational reconciliation:** compare records, logs, references, and states to identify the source of a discrepancy.
3. **Facilitated review:** allow affected participants to present information to a designated review process.
4. **Governance review:** refer disputes concerning shared rules or decisions to the relevant governance domain.
5. **Institutional referral:** refer questions requiring an external institution's authority to that institution or its applicable process.
6. **Legal process:** preserve the ability of affected parties to use competent legal procedures where legal rights or obligations are involved.

The network must not present its internal process as a substitute for a court, civil registry, regulator, or other legally competent authority.

### 12.5.3. Dispute Submission

A dispute submission should identify:

- The affected operation, record, decision, or protocol.
- The relevant references and versions.
- The issue being raised.
- The submitter's relationship to the matter.
- The supporting evidence or explanation.
- The requested review or outcome.

Only information necessary for the review should be submitted, subject to applicable requirements.

### 12.5.4. Review and Response

A review process should:

- Acknowledge receipt.
- Identify the appropriate decision domain.
- Determine whether it has authority to examine the matter.
- Notify relevant participants where permitted and necessary.
- Preserve the disputed state while review is pending.
- Examine the information according to defined criteria.
- Record the reasoning and outcome.
- Identify any appeal or external referral path.

### 12.5.5. Outcomes

Possible outcomes include:

- No discrepancy found.
- Technical interpretation clarified.
- Record reference corrected.
- Network status changed.
- Entry marked as disputed.
- Entry superseded or amended under an authorized process.
- Matter referred to an institution.
- Matter remains unresolved due to insufficient evidence or authority.

An unresolved dispute must not be represented as resolved merely to maintain a uniform network state.

### 12.5.6. Impartiality and Review

Where feasible, a person or body responsible for deciding a dispute should not be the sole author of the challenged decision.

The process should disclose conflicts of interest and provide a reasoned record of the outcome.

### 12.5.7. Time and Procedural Fairness

Review procedures should define reasonable response periods, extensions, and the handling of urgent matters.

The process must balance timely resolution with the need to examine relevant evidence and allow affected participants to respond.

### 12.5.8. External Authority and Legal Rights

A network dispute procedure may resolve the meaning or status of a network record. It cannot determine the legal status of a birth or override a competent institution's decision unless an applicable legal framework grants that authority.

Participation in the network must not waive a person's right to seek remedies or review under applicable law.

## 12.6. Architectural Autonomy from Territorial Administrative Structures

This section establishes the meaning and limits of the project's objective of architectural autonomy.

The project seeks to develop a network architecture whose technical principles and protocols are not inherently confined to one territorial administrative system or one government-operated platform.

That objective must be distinguished from political independence, state sovereignty, legal immunity, or authority over civil registration.

### 12.6.1. Technical Autonomy

Technical autonomy refers to the capacity of the architecture to operate through defined protocols and independent participating nodes without requiring every operation to be controlled by one mandatory central intermediary.

It may include:

- Platform-independent specifications.
- Multiple participating nodes.
- Distributed communication functions.
- Multiple record structures or Ledgers.
- Defined mechanisms for interoperability.
- Governance processes that do not depend exclusively on one operator.

Technical autonomy is a property to be demonstrated by implementation and operational tests.

### 12.6.2. Institutional and Territorial Boundaries

The architecture may connect participants and systems across territorial boundaries, but its operation remains subject to applicable laws and valid institutional arrangements.

A node's technical autonomy does not confer legal sovereignty. A network's distributed governance does not create a public authority. A Ledger cannot unilaterally replace a civil-registration system.

The United Nations Charter recognizes the sovereign equality of UN Member States and addresses international cooperation and matters within domestic jurisdiction. Those principles apply in their relevant legal and institutional context; they do not themselves define the governance of this project.

Core Identification Nodes byLAEV should therefore pursue cooperation while respecting the distinct legal responsibilities of participating jurisdictions.

### 12.6.3. Cooperation Without Mandatory Central Dependence

The project investigates whether participants can communicate and coordinate without requiring every operation to depend on one global operator or intermediary.

This does not mean that all central services are prohibited or unnecessary.

A system may use gateways, discovery services, institutional registrars, trusted data sources, or specialized operators for defined functions. The relevant question is whether such dependencies are necessary, transparent, proportionate, and consistent with the intended architecture.

### 12.6.4. Multiple Forms of Authority

The architecture must distinguish:

- **Protocol authority:** authority to define or approve a technical protocol within the project's governance.
- **Operational authority:** responsibility for operating a node or service.
- **Source authority:** responsibility for information maintained by an originating participant or institution.
- **Institutional authority:** powers established by an organization's rules or mandate.
- **Legal authority:** authority recognized under applicable law.

These authorities must not be collapsed into one general concept of network authority.

### 12.6.5. Platform Independence

The project's foundational protocols and concepts should remain independent of any single software platform.

GitHub may serve as an initial collaboration and development environment. It is not the network's ultimate governance authority, global registry, or permanent technical foundation.

The project should maintain the ability to migrate documentation, source code, node representations, and operational functions to other suitable infrastructure.

### 12.6.6. Neutrality and Non-Interference

The architecture should not require participants to adopt one political, cultural, or institutional model as a condition of technical interoperability, except where a defined common rule is necessary for the function being performed.

Technical neutrality does not remove legal obligations or permit unlawful activity. It means the architecture should remain capable of relating legitimate differences while respecting applicable rules.

### 12.6.7. Conceptual Boundary

The project may describe its architecture as territorially interoperable, platform-independent, and operationally decentralized where those properties are demonstrated.

It should not claim legal sovereignty, immunity from national laws, or authority to override jurisdictional registration procedures.

## 12.7. Institutional and International Cooperation Framework

The Institutional and International Cooperation Framework defines how the project may engage with public institutions, international organizations, civil society, technical standards bodies, academic institutions, and other relevant participants.

### 12.7.1. Purpose

The purpose is to establish a basis for cooperation that allows the architecture to be examined, tested, and potentially used in appropriate institutional contexts.

The project should seek cooperation where it can contribute to clearly defined functions and where the participating institution has the authority and capacity to engage.

### 12.7.2. Cooperation Participants

Potential participants may include:

- Civil-registration authorities.
- National statistical offices.
- Public digital-service institutions.
- International organizations.
- Regional cooperation bodies.
- Academic and research institutions.
- Civil-society organizations.
- Technical standards organizations.
- Software and infrastructure providers.
- Community organizations and other relevant stakeholders.

Participation by any entity must not be implied without its express agreement.

### 12.7.3. Cooperation Principles

Cooperation should be governed by:

- Respect for applicable institutional mandates.
- Clear allocation of responsibilities.
- Defined purpose and scope.
- Transparency of the proposed work.
- Proportionate information exchange.
- Technical and semantic interoperability.
- Accountability and review.
- Recognition of institutional and jurisdictional differences.
- Documented agreements where required.

### 12.7.4. Cooperation Without Presumed Endorsement

A reference to an international organization, standard, or public framework does not imply that the organization endorses Core Identification Nodes byLAEV.

Consultation, technical alignment, participation in a pilot, and formal endorsement are different forms of engagement.

The project must describe each relationship accurately and publish no claim of institutional support without a verifiable basis and appropriate authorization.

### 12.7.5. Cooperation Agreements

Where a formal cooperation arrangement is required, it should define:

- The participating entities.
- The purpose and scope.
- Responsibilities and decision authority.
- Information to be exchanged.
- Applicable legal and data-protection conditions.
- Technical interfaces and standards.
- Costs and resource commitments.
- Duration and review conditions.
- Procedures for disputes and termination.
- The status and permitted use of resulting records.

The agreement must not imply a transfer of legal authority unless that transfer is lawful and expressly established.

### 12.7.6. Pilots and Institutional Evaluation

Institutional pilots should have a bounded purpose and evaluation plan.

A pilot should define:

- The jurisdictions or institutions involved.
- The event or record category in scope.
- The systems and participants connected.
- The data exchanged and its legal basis.
- The technical and operational responsibilities.
- The evaluation indicators.
- The conditions for suspension or termination.
- The process for reporting and learning from results.

A successful pilot demonstrates results in its tested environment. It does not establish universal legal acceptance or global scalability.

### 12.7.7. International Standards and Existing Frameworks

The project should monitor and evaluate relevant international frameworks, including civil-registration and vital-statistics standards, digital-government principles, data interoperability specifications, and privacy and human-rights instruments.

The United Nations Legal Identity Agenda provides an existing framework for cooperation connecting civil registration, vital statistics, and related record-management functions.

The OECD Recommendation on Human-Centred Public Administrative Services, adopted in 2024, provides current guidance on designing public administrative services around people's needs and public value.

The OECD Recommendation on Digital Government Strategies also addresses transparency, openness, inclusion, stakeholder engagement, and digital exclusion. These frameworks are relevant references, but their recommendations apply within their stated institutional scope and do not automatically govern this independent project.

The project must identify where it aligns with existing standards, where it proposes a complementary function, and where additional research or agreements would be necessary.

### 12.7.8. Cooperation Does Not Require Institutional Uniformity

Participating institutions may retain different laws, administrative structures, languages, technical systems, and internal procedures.

Cooperation requires agreement on the specific definitions, interfaces, and responsibilities needed for an intended operation. It does not necessarily require institutional merger or identical internal processes.

### 12.7.9. International Cooperation Record

Each material institutional or international cooperation relationship should be documented with:

- The participating entity.
- The verified form of engagement.
- The scope and purpose.
- The applicable agreement or public reference.
- The responsibilities accepted by each party.
- The information or technical functions involved.
- The duration and current status.
- Any limitations on public description of the relationship.

This prevents exploratory discussions from being represented as formal adoption or endorsement.

## 12.8. Governance Structure and Decision Architecture

The seven governance domains require a clear organizational model. This section defines a reference structure for further development, without establishing permanent bodies or final voting arrangements.

### 12.8.1. Foundational Governance Layer

This layer maintains the project's foundational documents, principles, definitions, and core equation.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Maintaining the foundational writing standard.
- Reviewing changes to foundational principles.
- Preserving conceptual consistency.
- Documenting the rationale for material amendments.
- Identifying dependencies between foundational documents.

Changes at this layer require a defined and demanding review procedure because they may affect the entire project.

### 12.8.2. Standards and Protocol Governance Layer

This layer maintains shared technical and operational specifications.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Reviewing protocol proposals.
- Maintaining protocol versions and registries.
- Evaluating compatibility.
- Establishing conformance criteria.
- Coordinating protocol deprecation and migration.
- Publishing technical decisions and their rationale.

Its authority is limited to the protocols and standards under its defined mandate.

### 12.8.3. Operational Governance Layer

This layer concerns the operation of participating nodes, services, and Ledger infrastructure.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Operational reporting.
- Service continuity.
- Incident and discrepancy handling.
- Implementation conformance.
- Maintenance and version coordination.
- Operational performance evaluation.

Operational governance does not automatically have authority to change foundational principles or make legal determinations.

### 12.8.4. Institutional Cooperation Layer

This layer coordinates relationships with institutions and organizations participating under their own mandates.

Its responsibilities may include:

- Defining cooperation scopes.
- Maintaining institutional agreements.
- Coordinating pilots.
- Recording responsibilities and data-exchange conditions.
- Supporting institutional evaluation.
- Communicating results without overstating endorsement.

### 12.8.5. Independent Review Function

The architecture should provide for independent examination of significant claims, protocols, and implementations.

Reviewers should be selected for relevant competence and should disclose material conflicts of interest.

Independent review does not automatically confer governance authority. Its function is to provide evidence, findings, and recommendations to the decision body responsible for the relevant domain.

### 12.8.6. Separation of Duties

Where feasible, the roles of proposal author, technical implementer, approver, operator, and independent reviewer should be distinguishable.

The purpose is not to create bureaucracy for every action. It is to avoid concentrating all authority over a consequential decision in one role when independent examination is appropriate.

Small or early-stage project activities may combine roles, but such combinations should be visible and proportionate to the decision's significance.

### 12.8.7. Decision-Domain Matrix

| Decision domain | Primary responsibility | Required basis |
|---|---|---|
| Foundational principles | Foundational governance process | Documented reasoning and formal review |
| Concepts and definitions | Conceptual and standards review | Terminological consistency and impact analysis |
| Protocol specifications | Protocol governance process | Requirements, compatibility, and conformance review |
| Technical implementation | Assigned technical maintainers | Approved requirements and test results |
| Node operations | Node operators within their roles | Operational rules and service responsibilities |
| Shared Ledger state | Defined protocol participants | Applicable consensus rules |
| Institutional records | Source institution or competent authority | Applicable institutional and legal authority |
| Cross-border cooperation | Participating institutions | Valid agreements and applicable law |

The matrix is a reference allocation. The final governance model must identify the actual roles, bodies, and procedures that carry these responsibilities.

## 12.9. Transparency, Accountability, and Public Documentation

Governance decisions must be traceable and their scope understandable.

### 12.9.1. Public Governance Information

Subject to legitimate confidentiality and legal restrictions, the project should publish:

- Foundational documents and approved versions.
- Governance and participation rules.
- Protocol specifications and change histories.
- Material proposals and decision records.
- Conformance and evaluation reports.
- The status of institutional cooperation.
- Known limitations and unresolved matters.

Transparency concerns the rules, responsibilities, and outcomes of governance. It does not require public disclosure of personal or restricted records.

### 12.9.2. Decision Traceability

Each significant decision should be traceable to:

- The initiating issue or proposal.
- The evidence and reasoning considered.
- The participants or roles involved.
- The procedure used.
- The outcome and rationale.
- The affected documents or components.
- The effective date and review conditions.

### 12.9.3. Accountability of Governance Roles

Every governance role should have a defined scope and a way to report on its decisions and activities.

A role should not have unspecified or unlimited authority. The scope of any delegated authority must be documented and reviewable.

### 12.9.4. Review of Governance Performance

The governance system itself should be evaluated periodically.

Possible evaluation dimensions include:

- Accessibility of participation.
- Distribution of decision influence.
- Timeliness and quality of decisions.
- Transparency of reasoning.
- Frequency and resolution of disputes.
- Protocol stability and compatibility.
- Institutional cooperation outcomes.
- Evidence of exclusion or concentration of control.

Evaluation results may lead to changes in governance rules, subject to the appropriate approval process.

## 12.10. Governance Principles and Validation Criteria

### GP-01 — Distributed Participation

Participants may contribute to governance within defined roles and decision domains.

**Validation:** Governance rules identify eligibility, permitted activities, decision scope, and responsibilities.

### GP-02 — Explicit Authority Boundaries

Technical, operational, institutional, and legal authority must remain distinguishable.

**Validation:** Every governance function identifies its source of authority and limits.

### GP-03 — Proportionate Decision Rules

Decision procedures must be appropriate to the significance and scope of the decision.

**Validation:** The decision rule and threshold are documented and justified for each domain.

### GP-04 — Transparent Decision Records

Significant decisions must have traceable records and documented reasoning.

**Validation:** Reviewers can reconstruct the proposal, process, participants, result, and rationale within the permitted disclosure scope.

### GP-05 — Participant Accountability

Responsibilities must be explicit and proportionate to the roles participants undertake.

**Validation:** Each role has defined duties, reporting expectations, and review procedures.

### GP-06 — Fair Discrepancy Resolution

Disputes must be handled through defined processes that preserve unresolved differences and respect external authority.

**Validation:** The process identifies jurisdiction, review scope, evidence, decision authority, and referral or appeal routes.

### GP-07 — Controlled Protocol Evolution

Protocol changes must be reviewed, versioned, and accompanied by compatibility and migration analysis.

**Validation:** Every material change has a traceable proposal, approval record, and conformance assessment.

### GP-08 — Institutional Complementarity

Network governance must not claim legal authority that belongs to an external institution or jurisdiction.

**Validation:** Network statuses and decisions remain distinguishable from civil-registration and other legal determinations.

### GP-09 — Platform and Operational Independence

No single platform should become the unexamined permanent source of governance authority.

**Validation:** Governance records, protocol specifications, and decision procedures can be maintained independently of the initial development platform.

### GP-10 — Accountable International Cooperation

Institutional relationships must be accurately represented and supported by documented engagement or agreements.

**Validation:** Public claims about cooperation match the verified form, scope, and status of each relationship.

### GP-11 — Inclusion and Accessibility

Governance arrangements should reduce unnecessary barriers and avoid making participation in one digital platform a condition for access to legally established processes.

**Validation:** Participation and service models are examined for exclusion, accessibility, and alternative participation routes.

### GP-12 — Reviewability of Governance

The governance framework itself must remain open to examination and justified revision.

**Validation:** Periodic evaluation and a documented process for proposing governance changes exist.

## 12.11. Governance Argument and Conditions of Validity

The governance framework can be expressed through the following argument.

**Premise G-01:** The proposed ecosystem connects independent participants and systems that may have different roles, capacities, and institutional responsibilities.

**Premise G-02:** Shared protocols and record structures require decisions concerning participation, rule-making, implementation, and dispute resolution.

**Premise G-03:** Technical consensus can coordinate defined system states but does not independently create institutional or legal authority.

**Premise G-04:** Governance that does not define eligibility, authority, accountability, and review can produce ambiguity or concentration of control.

**Premise G-05:** Existing international principles recognize the relevance of cooperation, institutional boundaries, and the responsibilities of public authorities within their applicable scope.

**Inference:** The ecosystem requires a governance framework that distributes participation while defining decision domains, responsibilities, authority boundaries, and procedures for cooperation and review.

**Conclusion:** Core Identification Nodes byLAEV should adopt a documented, domain-specific, accountable, and reviewable governance architecture that supports distributed participation without claiming authority over external institutions or jurisdictions.

**Conditions of validity:** The framework must be implemented through explicit rules, tested in actual decision processes, evaluated for participation and accountability, and revised where evidence identifies weaknesses.

This conclusion justifies a governance architecture; it does not prove that a specific committee structure, voting model, or consensus threshold is already optimal.

## 12.12. Open Governance Questions

The following questions remain unresolved and require further work:

1. Which participant categories should have proposal, review, operational, or decision rights in each governance domain?
2. How should the project distinguish an individual participant from multiple technical accounts or nodes associated with that participant?
3. Which decisions require individual participation, institutional representation, technical maintainer approval, or more than one decision domain?
4. What voting or consensus models are appropriate for foundational, protocol, and operational decisions?
5. What thresholds should apply to different classes of change?
6. How should conflicts of interest and concentrated influence be monitored?
7. Which roles should be permanent, elected, delegated, rotating, or appointed?
8. What appeal or review process should apply to participation restrictions and governance decisions?
9. How should the project handle emergency changes without creating permanent exceptional authority?
10. Which institutional agreements are required before pilot exchanges of birth-event information?
11. How should governance work when a participant's local law or institutional mandate conflicts with a proposed network operation?
12. Which governance activities should be maintained directly in the GitHub repository during the initial development phase?
13. What measures will demonstrate that participation is genuinely distributed rather than merely open in theory?
14. How should the governance framework evolve as the project moves from documentation to prototypes and institutional pilots?

These questions should be answered through the project's governance design process rather than by presuming that one governance model is suitable for every phase and function.

## 12.13. Conclusion

The Governance Framework of Core Identification Nodes byLAEV establishes how distributed participation can coexist with explicit decision rules, defined responsibilities, controlled protocol evolution, discrepancy resolution, architectural autonomy, and institutional cooperation.

It distinguishes technical consensus from governance decisions and both from legal or institutional recognition. It treats participants as actors with differentiated roles rather than assuming that every node or account has identical authority. It requires transparency of rules and decisions while preserving appropriate restrictions on personal and institutional information.

The project seeks an architecture that can operate across territorial and technological boundaries without depending on one mandatory central intermediary for every function. This objective does not confer legal sovereignty, immunity from applicable laws, or authority to replace civil-registration institutions.

A coherent governance architecture must therefore be distributed where appropriate, accountable in every decision domain, complementary to legitimate institutional responsibilities, and open to independent examination and justified revision.

The governing principle is:

**Distributed participation requires defined authority, transparent decisions, accountable responsibilities, and cooperation across independent systems.**

**Core Identification Nodes byLAEV — Governance for a decentralized and interoperable civilizational network.**
